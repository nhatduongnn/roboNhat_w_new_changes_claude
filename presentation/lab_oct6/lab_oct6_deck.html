Posteriors above one

RoboCOP's decoder reports a probability of 1.027 × 1013 at some positions. Three parts of our pipeline each cope with that differently, and all three are wrong in at least one case.

What follows: the tuning runs that raised the background emission, the one locus that looked wrong, how posterior decoding is supposed to work and what our code does instead, the fix, and a check that the fix works.

Part 3 The posterior bug, in short

Two ways to stop underflow in the C code

α and β are products of thousands of small probabilities. Left alone, they drop below the smallest number a double can hold (4.9 × 10-324) and become 0.

1 · Scaling  ← RoboCOP

Rescale every position so the numbers stay near 1, then undo the scaling at the end.

2 · Log space

Store log p and add instead of multiplying. Nothing underflows, and there is nothing to undo.

Where it went wrong. Before today's fix, RoboCOP undid the scaling by multiplying about 5,000 ratios along the segment, instead of dividing each position by its row sum. A leftover factor stayed in the stored posterior: in bo09, rows over chrXIV 412,001–413,407 summed to 1.03 × 1013, not 1. The viewer then clipped each value to [0, 1], which drew the 0.50 on slide 3.

sr[i-1] = sr[i] * (sb[i] / sf[i]); ← algo.c calc_sr: ~5,000 multiplications, no per-position normalisation

Fix B · production

Divide each position by its row sum. Now in librobocop.so.

Log space · reference

Exact, including the emissions. Compared with fix B on slides 7–9.

H1

Part 3 Background

What posterior decoding computes

At every base, we want the probability that the genome is in each hidden state, given all the data — not just the data to the left.

Forward · α

Probability of everything seen up to position t, ending in state i.

Backward · β

Probability of everything after position t, given you start in state i.

γt(i) = αt(i) · βt(i) / Σj αt(j) · βt(j)

γ is what we plot and score. "ABF1 bound here with probability 0.95" is a γ value. By construction it sums to 1 across states at every position.

H2

Part 3 The classic problem

α and β underflow

Each step multiplies by more probabilities, all below 1. After a few hundred positions the numbers fall below what a double can hold and become exactly zero.

α after 500 positions ≈ 10-400 → 0.0 in double precision double precision bottoms out at 4.9 × 10-324

Every HMM implementation has to deal with this. It is underflow, not overflow — and RoboCOP does handle it.

H3

Part 3 The two standard cures

Scaling, or logs

1 · Scaling  ← RoboCOP

After each step, divide the row by its sum so it adds to 1. Remember the divisor. The numbers stay near 1, and you undo the scaling at the end.

2 · Log space

Store logarithms and add instead of multiplying. Nothing ever underflows, and there is nothing to undo.

RoboCOP uses scaling — normalize() in algo.c, applied to the forward table and the backward table.

The bug is not in the scaling. It is in the undo.

H4

Part 3 Why the textbook form is safe

The scale factors cancel

Suppose we scaled the forward row by any number A and the backward row by any number B:

α̂ = α / A β̂ = β / B α̂·β̂ / Σ α̂·β̂ = (α·β / AB) / (Σ α·β / AB) = α·β / Σ α·β = γ

A and B cancel exactly, whatever they were. Dividing each position by its own row sum gives the right answer without ever needing to know the scale factors.

This is an identity, not an approximation.

The drift scales every entry by the same factor, so the shape survives. Dividing by the row sum is the only one of the three that recovers it.

H6

Part 3 What our code does

It rebuilds the scale instead of cancelling it

Rather than dividing by the row sum, RoboCOP reconstructs the undo factor by multiplying ratios along the whole segment:

/* algo.c — calc_sr */
sr[len-1] = 1;
for (i = len-1; i > 0; i--) {
    sr[i-1] = sr[i] * (sb[i] / sf[i]);          ← 5,000 multiplications
//  if (isinf(sr[i-1])) sr[i-1] = DBL_MAX;      ← guard, commented out
//  sr[i-1] = sr[i] + log(sb[i]) - log(sf[i]);  ← log version, abandoned
}

Then posterior = (sb·β) · (sr·α), with no normalisation anywhere.

One bad ratio contaminates every position upstream of it. Note sr is declared long double while everything else is double — the type was widened on purpose to delay this.

H7

Part 3 What it looks like in our data

One bad ratio, 1,407 poisoned positions

Row sums from a single segment of chrXIV in the bo09 decode:

chrXIV 412,001 – 413,407 → 1.02705 × 1013 (constant) chrXIV 413,408 → 5.09657 × 1012 chrXIV 413,409 onward → 1.0 (correct)

Three values, one jump. Not gradual noise — a single constant applied to a whole run of positions, exactly as the sr product predicts. Which is also why dividing by the row sum recovers it perfectly.

Across the run: 20 of 400 segments affected, 7,139 positions. Other campaigns with milder weights: 14 positions, or zero.

H9

Part 3 Why now

Fiber-seq exercises what MNase never did

A single Fiber-seq emission term, binomial at 67 reads:

Modelk = 0k = 34k = 67
Nucleosome · p = 0.0464.2 × 10-21.3 × 10-273.9 × 10-90
Background · p = 0.2494.7 × 10-93.3 × 10-63.5 × 10-41

Two channels multiply at every position: worst case 1.5 × 10-179 at a single base, against a floating-point floor of 4.9 × 10-324.

Extreme tuned concentrations widen it further. Upstream, with MNase, only ever saw values slightly above 1 — which is exactly what their code comments say.

H10

Part 3 Is it a bug, or intended?

The paper says probability

"RoboCOP performs posterior decoding and yields the probability of each DBF at every position in the genome. The score on the y-axis is the probability of that location being bound by a given DBF." Mitra, Zhong, Tran, MacAlpine & Hartemink · NAR 49:7925 (2021) · Figure 2E

Nothing in the paper or supplement says the output may exceed 1, is unnormalised, or is a relative score. And the code carries the authors' own tripwires:

  • A warning that fires above 1.000000001 — nine decimal places of tolerance.
  • if np.sum(p_table) > 1e10: continue — upstream discards exactly our case.
  • // some scores are slightly > 1 due to numerical issues — "slightly" is the tell.
  • Plot axes pinned to ylim(0, 1).

Verdict: a latent upstream defect, and our emissions are what woke it up.

H11

Part 4 The fix

Divide by the row sum

rs = dp[:, :silent_states_begin].sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) dp = dp / rs

Three places it can go, and we are testing all three against each other on the same run:

A · on read

Patch where the posterior is loaded. Fixes every existing decode, no recompile. Temporary.

B · in C, on write

Same formula where the posterior is produced. Reaches every consumer, including upstream's own plotting.

C · log space

Finish the rewrite the authors abandoned. Nothing to undo, nothing to drift. The real repair.

Each on its own branch, each decoding the same bv75 run in all three layer configurations. If the three disagree beyond rounding, the assumption that this is a clean scale factor is wrong — and that is worth knowing before we trust the retrofit.

Why rDNA differs: at thousands of reads the binomial emission underflows to 0, and the code floors it to 10-30. That flips the evidence: at 4,379 reads with no m6A, the most-protected state should win by 10929; after the floor it loses by 1017. Log space computes the emission exactly.

TakeawayWhere coverage is normal, the two decoders agree. On high-coverage rDNA, only log space is exact. Neither rDNA row is a believable ABF1 map; the point is that they disagree.
