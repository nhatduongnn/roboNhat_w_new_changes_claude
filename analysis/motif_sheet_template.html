Which of the three is the odd one out?

Three datasets measure the same yeast factors: the native set RoboCOP decodes with, JASPAR, and Rossi. None is a reference — none is more authoritative than the others. So the question is asked symmetrically. With three points there is always a closest pair, and the odd one out is the member opposite it — asked once per Rossi replicate, so a verdict can be replicated rather than asserted, with heat-shocked replicates labelled and held out. Distances are TOMTOM's own column functions reported raw, as a mean per column — no p-values, and all three are measured on one common window per row so they can be compared with each other.

Reading the three numbers

Each row carries three pairwise distances, all measured on the same window. Two datasets agree when they sit at or below KL 0.060 — not a guess, but drawn from the data: over the 136 replicate rows where all three exist, the closest pair has median distance 0.032 and p75 0.073, so 0.060 is what "the two that agree best" typically costs. It falls at the 70th percentile of that distribution, close to where it fell before the common window was imposed, so the threshold did not need re-tuning. The odd one out must then be at least 0.030 further out than that closest pair, or the call is marked ambiguous rather than forced.

The symmetric view changes the story. Distance to the other two runs jaspar 0.142 · native 0.160 · rossi 0.174 (medians; means 0.290 / 0.302 / 0.461). Rossi is the most out of step of the three, and it is the odd one out for 29 native motifs against the native set's 7. Treating JASPAR and Rossi jointly as ground truth would have been wrong.

That does not rescue the seven where the native set is the outlier — those are still real, and ABF1 and all four RAP1 matrices are among them, both factors with fitted Fiber-seq parameters. ABF1 and three of the RAP1 matrices are unanimous across every replicate, on 195–200 sites.

A threshold caveat worth reading. no consensus fires whenever even the closest pair exceeds 0.060. That is an absolute test and it does not scale with the size of the gap — a row can have one pair four times closer than the other two and still not be called. Of the 22 no consensus motifs, 11 have native as the furthest out. That is recorded on every row and shown on hover, so a near miss is not the same as an exoneration.

One row per Rossi replicate

JASPAR ships one matrix per gene. Rossi runs each factor as several independent ChExMix samples — up to 6 — each yielding up to three motifs. Collapsing those to a single "best" matrix threw away the most valuable thing in the data: the replication. So each native motif gets one row per Rossi replicate, and the same verdict reached independently in every replicate is far stronger evidence than any single pick could be.

It matters. Abf1_murphy and three of the four RAP1 matrices come out native-is-odd in every replicate, unanimously, as does Pdr1_badis over its two. Cad1_murphy manages it in only 1 of 2 — same headline verdict, far weaker evidence, and the replicate support column is what separates them.

Statistical power is on every row. The median surviving Rossi motif rests on just 28 sites and 10% on fewer than 10 — MEME caps the count at 200. A large distance from a 9-site motif is noise; the same distance from a 195-site motif is a finding. Rows are tagged weak (<30), moderate (30–99) or strong (≥100). Every native-is-odd call rests on at least 57 sites, and the ABF1 and RAP1 ones — the Fiber-fitted pair — on 195–200.

Growth condition — and heat shock

The <id>_YEP directory name is the Yeast Epigenome Project tag, not a growth medium, and the downloaded bundles carry no metadata at all — their README lists only file formats. The condition has to come from the deposit. It does: GEO GSE147927 carries a treatment: field per sample, and the lab's sample-key.tab ties sample IDs to GEO accessions.

Rossi grew cells in YPD to OD600 0.8 at 25 °C, then heat-shocked a subset by adding an equal volume of 55 °C medium to reach 37 °C for 3 or 6 minutes. Of 1236 deposited samples, 22 are heat shock — and 21 of them are on this disk, across Hsf1, Rpb3, Rsc1/3/6/9/58, Sgf11, Spt7, Spt15, Spt16, Sth1, Tpk2 and Ubp8.

Five rows of this sheet use one, and they are not incidental: both of HSF1's later replicates, both of RSC3's, and one of SPT15's. They are shown and labelled 3′ HS / 6′ HS but excluded from the replicate consensus — a motif measured at 37 °C can differ from a 25 °C one for real biological reasons, which is a different claim from "somebody's matrix is wrong". Excluding SPT15's heat-shocked replicate moves it from 4-of-4 to 3-of-3; its verdict is unchanged.

Rebuild the condition table any time with analysis/fetch_rossi_conditions.py, which pulls both sources and cross-checks that all 1236 sample IDs agree.

Why there is no universal E-value cutoff

A MEME E-value is the expected number of motifs scoring as well in a shuffled dataset of the same size. It therefore measures evidence within one run, and scales with how much data that run had — log10E tracks log10 of the site count at r = −0.54. Across the 302 genes with a Rossi motif, the best E-value ranges from 1e−500 down to 1e−1.4. Any single threshold either keeps junk for the data-rich factors or throws away the real motif of the sparse ones. So the answer to "is there a universal threshold" is no — and it is not even stable between replicates of one TF, whose best E-values span a median of 8.5 orders of magnitude.

A relative rule works where an absolute one cannot, which is your "clearly better by multiple folds" instinct, made concrete. Within a single replicate the top motif is taken outright when it leads the runner-up by at least 2 orders of magnitude; 87% of multi-motif samples clear that, with a median lead of 10.3 orders. When they do not clear it, the runner-up is not discarded — it gets its own row, marked 2nd best, so a genuine tie is shown as a tie rather than silently resolved.

One window per row, so the three numbers are comparable

The three matrices are rarely the same width. RAP1 is native 20 / JASPAR 12 / Rossi 16. Align each pair on its own terms and the row's three numbers rest on different column counts — 12, 16 and 12 for that row — and then "which pair is closest" can be settled by how many columns a pair happened to share rather than by the matrices themselves. So every distance in a row is measured on one window: the columns that all the matrices present actually reach. The window column carries that count; hover it for the three widths.

Why not simply align each pair the way TOMTOM would and intersect? That is the obvious approach, and where it works this reduces to exactly it. It does not always work, because alignment is not transitive: fixing JASPAR against native and Rossi against native already fixes JASPAR against Rossi, and there is no guarantee it lands where the JASPAR↔Rossi optimum wants it. Across the sheet the three pairwise optima are mutually realisable in a single frame in only 82 of 140 rows (59%); in the rest the frame costs the JASPAR↔Rossi pair a median of 0.078 KL and up to 1.52. TOMTOM cannot arbitrate — it compares one query motif against target motifs and has no notion of three motifs at once; there is no multiple-motif alignment anywhere in the MEME suite.

So the frame is chosen in one joint search rather than stitched from three pairwise answers: all offsets and orientations, scored by the sum of the three pairwise distances — a sum-of-pairs alignment, symmetric in the datasets. On the 82 rows where the naive approach is well defined, the two agree on 78 verdicts; the four that differ are all cases where this one is right and the pairwise-stitched frame would have been the compromise.

Two details of the search. It runs against a fixed window the width of the shortest matrix, with the columns a matrix cannot reach priced against the genome background, so that a matrix is free to sit where it belongs but cannot buy a better score by shrinking the window onto the columns that happen to match. The reported numbers are then taken over the columns everyone reaches. That freedom matters: ABF1's third Rossi replicate is registered two columns over from the other two, and pinning it inside a 14-column window drove its distance from 0.17 to 1.03 and broke an otherwise unanimous call. It now scores on 13 columns with the fourteenth dropped, and the call holds.

Two things this costs, both stated on the rows rather than buried. First, extra width is invisible to the distance: RAP1's native matrix is eight columns longer than JASPAR's, and those eight columns — a real G-rich tail neither other dataset reports — contribute nothing to 0.286. The logo is where you see them. Second, the window is often short: many native matrices are 5–8 columns wide, so 120 of 213 rows compare on fewer than 8 columns, and that is marked in amber. A short window is a weak measurement whichever way the verdict falls. A −n tag means n columns of the shortest matrix fell outside another one and were dropped; 44 rows drop one or two, none drops more.
